Put It to Work

The operational side — asset classification, incident escalation, stakeholder communication.

Asset classification Escalation criteria Stakeholder communication

Protecting data and communicating incidents

Everyday responsibilities:

  • Alert triage in the SIEM — separate signal from noise.
  • Log review across auth, network, endpoint and cloud sources.
  • Running playbooks step-by-step for known incident types.
  • Ticket hygiene — every alert gets a clean audit trail.
  • Escalation when the incident is outside my scope or severity level.

Classify assets by sensitivity and map controls to each class: public, internal, confidential, restricted. Higher classification → stronger controls (encryption, MFA, more frequent access review).

Access reviews — who has access to what, is it still needed, is it least privilege? Do them on a defined cadence.

Reliability, availability, security (RAS) trade-offs — every added control costs something (latency, cost, usability). The right answer balances risk reduction with operational cost.

Escalating incidents

Escalation = passing an incident up the chain (or across teams) when its severity, scope or complexity exceeds my role’s authority.

Escalation criteria:

  • Severity — potential business impact if not contained. Common tiers: Critical / High / Medium / Low.
  • Scope — how many users / hosts / systems are affected.
  • Sensitivity — is PII, PHI, cardholder data, IP or a privileged account involved?
  • Regulatory impact — does GDPR / HIPAA / PCI DSS require notification?

Who to escalate to: SOC lead / tier 2, IR team, legal, PR / communications, HR (for insider incidents), executive leadership for a Critical.

Anatomy of a good escalation ticket — one glance should answer:

  • What happened (concise summary).
  • Scope & affected assets.
  • What is already known — IoCs, timeline, first-detected timestamp.
  • What has already been done — containment steps, verifications.
  • What I need from the escalation path — decisions, additional access, communication approval.

Incident handler’s journal — timestamped notes of every action during the incident. Feeds the post-incident report and preserves chain of custody.

Communicating effectively with stakeholders

Stakeholders to deal with: management (VPs, CISO), engineering, product, legal / compliance, HR, customer support, customers, regulators, external partners / auditors.

Tailor the message to the audience:

  • Executive summary — impact, timeline, decisions needed, cost, next steps. No jargon.
  • Technical write-up — IoCs, packet-level detail, log evidence, remediation steps. For peer analysts and engineers.
  • Customer-facing — plain language, empathetic, factual, no unnecessary internals.

Written comms: incident summary, executive brief, playbook updates, post-mortem, weekly SOC report.

Verbal comms: shift stand-up, incident bridge, hand-off between shifts, tabletop exercise.

Difficult conversations: delivering bad news to a customer or executive; holding a boundary on scope; saying “we don’t know yet” without losing credibility.

Ethical duty of care: don’t overclaim, don’t hide facts, respect the privacy of affected users.

Pitfalls I stay aware of

  • Under-escalating a Critical incident to avoid noise — wastes the head-start you have on containment.
  • Over-escalating routine noise — erodes trust in your judgement and clogs the escalation channel.
  • Writing an escalation ticket that’s just “something is wrong” — the receiving analyst has to redo all the triage. Always include the 5 W’s and what you’ve already tried.
  • Mixing exec and technical audiences in the same message — write two.

Broader concepts I’m fluent in that expand on the above

  • Post-mortem discipline — every real incident gets a blame-free write-up, root-cause analysis, corrective actions with owners, and a follow-up date.
  • Runbook / playbook maintenance — playbooks aren’t “write once”; every incident should improve the next version.
  • Metrics that show a SOC is maturing — MTTD, MTTR, false-positive rate, percent of alerts closed by tier 1 without escalation, time-to-contain.
  • Cross-functional collaboration — my RAN background already involves live incident coordination across vendor, macro, core and IT teams; that collaboration muscle transfers directly to SOC work with legal, engineering, product and PR.
