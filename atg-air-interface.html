ATG Air Interface — 800 MHz LTE Air-to-Ground

Gogo's 800 MHz Air-to-Ground (ATG) network is an LTE air interface built for a single purpose — delivering in-flight connectivity from sparse ground base-stations up to aircraft at cruise altitude. The combination of narrowband spectrum, a single-frequency network, long inter-site distance and an aircraft-shaped UE makes almost every classic LTE tuning instinct point the wrong way. This page captures how I think about the ATG air interface and why terrestrial LTE habits have to be re-grounded before using them here.

800 MHz narrowband Single-frequency network 100+ km inter-site Low grazing angles · high Doppler Aircraft-as-UE

Band & bandwidth — narrow on purpose

  • Downlink: ~9 RB near 850 MHz (approximately 1.6 MHz of usable bandwidth).
  • Uplink: ~6 RB near 895 MHz (~1.08 MHz).
  • Frequency band: ~800 MHz — sub-GHz for long range and favourable Fresnel-zone clearance from ground sites to aircraft at cruise altitude.
  • Spectrum is scarce — every RB counts. A single bad PCI plan or a single cell leaking interference at the edge visibly degrades throughput for every aircraft in the region.

Because the carrier is narrow, the usual “throw more bandwidth” tool from terrestrial LTE is off the table. Headroom comes from cleaner SINR, better mobility, and better MIMO rank, not from a wider carrier.

Single-frequency network — every cell is a co-channel interferer

Terrestrial LTE/5G networks typically reuse multiple carriers across neighbouring cells. On ATG, there is only one carrier, so every neighbour cell is co-channel. At cruise altitude an aircraft sees many ground cells simultaneously; whichever one isn’t serving the UE is contributing to the interference floor.

  • Interference-limited by design. SINR, not RSRP, is the operating quantity.
  • Dominance matters. With no frequency separation, the only way to get clean SINR is for one cell to dominate the others at the aircraft’s position (through geometry, tilt and power).
  • PCI collisions bite harder. On 2-TX cells, PCI mod-3 collisions put CRS and PSS on the same REs, corrupting channel estimation for both cells. See PCI Planning for 2-TX ATG Cells.

Sparse long-range ground sites

Ground base-stations on the ATG network are spaced 100+ km apart to blanket the continental US with a few hundred sites. That gives several consequences the air interface is designed around:

  • Low grazing angles. From an aircraft at cruise the elevation angle to a neighbour cell can be a few degrees — shallow enough that polarization, pointing and aircraft attitude all matter.
  • Long propagation delays. PRACH preamble format, Ncs / zero-correlation-zone and cell-radius parameters are all stretched beyond terrestrial defaults. See UL Random Access & PRACH for ATG.
  • Few, large cells. Handover rate is lower than terrestrial but each handover is a bigger deal — the UE has fewer fallbacks if one goes wrong.

High Doppler & aircraft dynamics

  • Doppler shifts at ~800 MHz are moderate but non-trivial at 500+ kt groundspeed; PRACH requires the high-speed / restricted set to avoid inter-preamble correlation.
  • Roll, bank and pitch rotate the aircraft antennas. Signal on the vertical and horizontal polarizations swaps rapidly during banking, producing transient fades that look like short SINR dips to the diagnostic chain.
  • Altitude changes LOS geometry. On climb and descent the serving-cell set rotates faster than cruise — mobility tuning has to accommodate both regimes.

See Flight-Test Analysis & Radio-vs-Attitude Behaviour for how these effects actually appear on the diagnostic chain.

The aircraft as the UE

  • Aircard = the LTE modem mounted on the aircraft, with external fuselage antennas. The aircraft, not a handset, is what the eNB sees.
  • Load-change testing therefore has two surfaces: the ground-station (GBSS) software load and the aircard firmware load. Both get validated in the lab before a flight. See ATG Lab Validation Methodology.
  • One UE, one flight, many cells. A single flight exercises dozens of handovers across the network — each one is a data point about network behaviour, not just UE behaviour.

Why terrestrial habits don’t transfer cleanly

  • “Just add a carrier” — there isn’t one. SINR, not BW, is the lever.
  • “Long TTT damps ping-pong” — it also lets the serving cell die before the handover fires. See High-Mobility Handover Tuning.
  • “PCI mod-6 is enough” — on 2-TX cells, mod-3 drives CRS/PSS collisions and matters more than mod-6 for interference.
  • “RSRP is king” — on a single-frequency network with no dominance, RSRP is often the same across cells and SINR/RSRQ do the discriminating.
  • “TCP will fill the pipe” — with long RTT and a modest MCS budget, TCP window / RTT caps throughput well below radio capability unless the server is close or the window is tuned. See TCP/UDP Throughput & the Radio-Transport Boundary.
