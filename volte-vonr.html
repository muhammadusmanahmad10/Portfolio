Voice: VoNR / VoLTE, EPS Fallback & SRVCC

5G voice rides on IMS as VoNR (Voice over New Radio). But not every network or device supports VoNR yet, and coverage isn't uniform — so the network needs graceful fallbacks. EPS Fallback moves a call from 5G NR to LTE VoLTE at setup; SRVCC hands an in-progress VoLTE call down to 3G/2G when LTE coverage is lost. I validate that voice continuity holds through all three.

EPS Fallback flow

In validation: I confirm the call is not dropped during fallback by tracing the RRC reconfiguration (NR→LTE) followed by the SIP INVITE flow on LTE, and I track EPS Fallback Success Rate.

SRVCC flow

SRVCC keeps an ongoing VoLTE call alive when LTE coverage ends and only 3G/2G CS voice is available: the eNB, MME and MSC coordinate a handover of the IMS voice call to the circuit-switched domain. In validation I trigger a VoLTE call on LTE, walk the UE into 3G-only coverage, and trace Measurement Report → SRVCC Handover Command → CS call setup in 3G, tracking SRVCC Success Rate and Call Drop Rate.

Where this fits my hands-on work

On the 5G SA Femto program I validated VoNR, ViNR (video), RTT, SMS over IMS and supplementary services (call waiting/forwarding/hold/resume), VoNR↔ViNR upgrade/downgrade, multi-party (3-way) calls, and simultaneous voice+video+data+SMS — plus IRAT voice continuity (VoNR↔VoLTE) and the rule that a non-VoNR device is barred from the VoNR-only femto (so its voice/911 proceeds on the macro layer). EPS Fallback and SRVCC are the LTE/legacy counterparts of that same voice-continuity discipline.

Case study — garbled voice from RTP delay / jitter

Symptom
On a VoNR call the audio was garbled / robotic — intelligible at the SIP level (call set up cleanly, RTP flowing both ways) but choppy on the listener side. No call drop, just poor speech quality.
Investigation
Captured the call end-to-end and ran Wireshark’s Telephony → RTP → Stream Analysis. SIP/SDP looked fine. At the RTP layer, however, the stream showed elevated inter-arrival jitter — packets were arriving with too much variation in spacing for the UE’s jitter buffer to smooth out. Some samples were also late enough to be discarded by the buffer as "arrived too late", which produces the characteristic robotic/garbled effect.
What was happening
The RTP packets weren’t lost in the strict sense — they were getting through, but the packet-delay variation exceeded the receiver’s buffer depth. Common contributors are: the voice 5QI 1 flow not actually being prioritised end-to-end (so RTP queues behind best-effort data under load), backhaul queueing during congestion, or a transport hop that bursts then idles.
Concept & mitigation
Voice quality on VoNR depends on three things: codec (a codec is the method used to compress voice) / packetisation (how much voice data is carried in each RTP packet) as negotiated in SDP, the 5QI 1 bearer being genuinely prioritised end-to-end (including DSCP marking on the transport), and a properly sized jitter buffer at the receiver. Fixes typically come from one of those three knobs — ensuring the voice flow rides 5QI 1 with the right DSCP across the transport, or revisiting the receive-side jitter buffer for the codec in use. Takeaway: "garbled voice" with no packet-loss alarm is almost always a jitter / delay-variation problem, not a codec or loss problem.

Case study — SMS MO/MT failing because the SIM’s iFC pointed at an older SMSC

Symptom
On the femto, IMS registration succeeded and VoNR calls worked, but SMS over IMS (MO and MT) was failing. The UE could attach and make calls, just not send or receive SMS.
Concept — iFC routing for SMS
iFC (initial Filter Criteria) is the IMS routing rule attached to a subscriber’s service profile in the HSS/UDM. When the S-CSCF processes a SIP message for that subscriber, it walks the iFCs in priority order; each iFC matches on SIP headers (method = MESSAGE, Request-URI, etc.) and forwards matching traffic to the configured application server (AS) — for SMS, that AS is the SMSC. If the iFC points at the wrong SMSC, MESSAGE traffic gets handed to a node that can’t deliver it.
Root cause
The subscriber’s iFC at SIM-provisioning was still pointing at an older SMSC instance that was being deprecated and was having issues handling new SIP MESSAGE traffic. By default the legacy provisioning template put every new subscriber’s SMS-iFC on this older SMSC, so MO MESSAGE requests landed there and never reached the new SMSC chain.
Fix
SIM-provisioning team updated the subscriber’s SMS iFC to point at the new SMSC. After re-provisioning and re-registration, SIP MESSAGE was routed to the correct SMS-AS and SMS MO/MT recovered. The SMS-SME owner separately migrated other subscribers off the legacy SMSC.
Concept & takeaway
SMS-over-IMS failures with healthy VoNR are almost always an IMS routing / iFC issue, not a radio issue — the call path works because voice iFCs are correct, but the SMS iFC’s AS target is wrong. Takeaway: when SMS fails and voice works, check the iFC chain in the subscriber’s HSS/UDM profile before blaming the air interface.

Case study — VoNR call-setup time across cell-centre / mid / edge

Method. Measured VoNR call-setup time at three RF positions (cell-centre, cell-mid, cell-edge) by sweeping attenuation, with 10 MO calls per position. For each call I captured XCAL5 SIP/IMS trace and timed from SIP INVITE (sent by the calling UE) to 180 Ringing received back — that’s the user-facing “phone is ringing” latency the customer perceives. Reported the per-position mean, min and max across the 10-call sample.

Why this matters: a single golden call at cell-centre isn’t enough — setup time grows under poorer RF because the UE needs more PRACH attempts, more RRC retransmissions and longer scheduling waits. A 10-call sweep across centre / mid / edge gives you a real per-position distribution so a regression in MCS / scheduler / SIP-handling shows up against the baseline.

Case study — UE1 hotspot coexistence (VoNR/ViNR on UE1 while UE2 streams over UE1’s Wi-Fi hotspot)

Scenario. UE1 was holding a VoNR / ViNR call on the femto and at the same time acting as a Wi-Fi hotspot; UE2 was tethered to UE1’s hotspot and running a heavy data session (download / browsing). The test exercised simultaneous cellular voice on UE1 and Wi-Fi-routed data on UE2 through UE1.

What I validated. On UE1: the VoNR/ViNR call stayed up with no audio gap, no glitching and no codec downgrade — verified end-to-end (SIP setup → RTP flow → clean release) and on the air-interface side. On UE2: the data session through UE1’s hotspot kept its throughput target with stable latency — no drops attributable to UE1’s cellular activity, and UE1’s Wi-Fi AP didn’t fall over.

Why this matters: hotspot coexistence is a common real-world configuration (e.g. a phone on a call while it tethers a laptop). The two paths share the UE’s CPU, RF front-end and battery budget, so if the cellular scheduler / Wi-Fi driver interaction is mis-tuned, one of them degrades silently. Confirming UE1 holds the voice flow and UE2 keeps its data throughput — both at the same time — is the right acceptance criterion.

Field notes

  • EPS Fallback = before media; SRVCC = during call. Different triggers, different KPIs.
  • Voice continuity is the success criterion — the call must survive the radio change with no audible gap.
  • VoNR needs SA + IMS + correct 5QI 1 QoS flow; missing any one sends the device to fallback.
