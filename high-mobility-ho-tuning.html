High-Mobility Handover Tuning — TTT & Hysteresis

On the ATG network, radio conditions degrade fast as the aircraft banks or passes cell-edge. The conventional terrestrial reflex is a long Time-to-Trigger (TTT) to damp ping-pong — but in high mobility that same long TTT lets the serving cell die before the handover command ever fires, causing a class of radio-link failures. The fix is a shorter TTT + A3 hysteresis margin: trigger fast enough to escape the degrading cell, and only trigger toward a target that is genuinely better. I diagnosed this pattern and recommended / validated the parameter change, which removed most of the RLFs.

A3 event Time-to-Trigger (TTT) Hysteresis margin 320 ms → 80 ms RLF reduction

The A3 event, in one place

A3 fires when a neighbour cell becomes an offset better than the serving cell for the duration of TTT. The decision in plain words:

  • Mn > Ms + Ofs + Ocn − Ocs + Hyst
  • Ms / Mn — serving and neighbour measurement quantity (RSRP or RSRQ).
  • Ofs / Ocs / Ocn — frequency and per-cell offsets.
  • Hyst (hysteresis) — a safety margin to avoid oscillation; must be sustained for TTT before the UE reports. 3GPP TS 36.331 defines the event.

TTT and hysteresis are the two knobs. On ATG they interact strongly with aircraft dynamics.

Why a long TTT breaks on a banking aircraft

Terrestrial networks often set TTT to several hundred milliseconds to filter out short fades. On an aircraft turning onto a new heading, the conditions evolve on the order of a few hundred milliseconds:

  • A 25° bank rotates the aircraft antennas visibly in <1 s.
  • Serving-cell RSRP can drop faster than the TTT window; by the time the condition has been sustained for 320 ms, the serving cell is already too weak for the HO command itself to be reliably received or for the UL re-establishment to succeed.
  • Result: radio-link failure fires (T310 expiry, then re-establishment that often fails because nothing is strong enough to answer the Msg3 re-establishment request on the new cell).

The fix — TTT 320 ms → 80 ms, plus hysteresis margin

Symptom
Repeated RLFs during banking turns and at cell-edge; downstream re-establishment failures with T311 expiry; connected-time % below target on multi-cell flight tracks.
Diagnosis
Mapped RLF timestamps against the A3-trigger → HO-command sequence in the flight logs. The A3 trigger was present but the HO command either never completed or arrived too late for the UE to execute on a receivable RF path. I correlated the RLF density with roll/bank angle and cell-edge geometry in the MATLAB plot set.
Change recommended & validated
  • TTT: 320 ms → 80 ms. HO decision now fires fast enough that the serving cell is still viable when the command is issued.
  • A3 hysteresis margin: introduced where previously 0 dB. Prevents triggering on a neighbour that is only transiently better and forces the UE toward a genuinely stronger target.
Outcome
Majority of the RLF cluster removed; connected-time %, HO-success % and handover-failure % all improved on subsequent flight campaigns. No noticeable ping-pong increase because the hysteresis margin absorbed short fluctuations.

Takeaway: on high-mobility ATG, the conventional “long TTT damps ping-pong” heuristic inverts. The right answer is short TTT + hysteresis — fire fast, fire only toward something actually better.

Why the two parameters must move together

  • Short TTT alone would trigger more handovers, including toward marginal neighbours, and create ping-pong.
  • Hysteresis alone doesn’t help if the TTT is still long enough that the serving cell dies first.
  • Together they say: react faster, but only commit to a target that is clearly better — which is exactly what the ATG geometry requires.

Related knobs on the ATG mobility chain

  • a3-Offset — shifts the whole decision toward later / earlier HO. Used alongside hysteresis; different mechanism (fixed offset vs safety margin).
  • Report-on-leave / cell-individual offsets — used selectively to bias toward or away from problem neighbours (e.g. mod-3-conflicted or known weak-coverage cells).
  • T310 / N310 / N311 — radio-link-failure timers; relevant because if TTT is too long, T310 fires first and the HO never happens. See RLF & Recovery on ATG.
  • T311 — re-establishment supervisor; if re-establishment fails before T311 expiry the UE drops into full connection setup, breaking the user session visibly.

Pitfalls I stay aware of

  • Treating ping-pong and RLF as the same problem. They’re opposite failure modes with opposite parameter responses.
  • Tuning TTT without tuning hysteresis. One without the other usually swaps failure modes rather than fixing them.
  • Not correlating RLFs with attitude. Many are bank-angle events masquerading as mobility faults.
  • Ignoring target-cell health at HO time. If the target is mod-3-conflicted with the serving cell, a fast HO can still fail. Mobility tuning only works on a clean PCI plan — see PCI Planning for 2-TX ATG Cells.
