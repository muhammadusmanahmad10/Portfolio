Load & Stress Testing, Throughput & KPIs

A cell that works for one UE can collapse under many. Load and stress testing pushes the femto to its limits — many simultaneous UEs, sustained traffic, congestion — to confirm it stays stable and that KPIs (RRC success, context setup, throughput, CPU/PRB) hold. I drive this with TM500, real handsets, and traffic tools (iPerf, Ookla, IXIA), and analyse the resulting KPI reports.

Case study — iPerf vs Ookla: why "throughput" depends on the tool

Setup
Three UEs sharing one femto, measuring downlink throughput distribution across them.
Observation
With Ookla speedtest the three UEs got an uneven share; with iPerf the same three UEs got a uniform distribution.
Why
Ookla opens bursty, server-selected TCP flows and measures peak per-device, so whichever UE grabs the scheduler first dominates. iPerf drives steady, controlled streams, so the scheduler shares PRBs fairly. Same network — different measurement semantics.

Takeaway: a "throughput problem" can be a measurement-methodology artefact. Knowing what each tool actually does prevents raising false RAN defects. (I confirmed real-world feel separately — an Instagram-feed scroll test on 3 UEs sustained ~70 Mbps DL / ~4 Mbps UL.)

Case study — IXIA backhaul policer (controlled 100 Mbps)

To test the femto under a constrained backhaul without changing the physical link, I configured a bandwidth limit on IXIA:

Config

  • Physical backhaul: 1 Gbps
  • Femto cap: 100 Mbps bi-directional
  • Downlink: policer, committed 100 Mbps, no excess
  • Uplink: shaper at 100 Mbps (smooth, fewer drops)
  • Small burst tolerance for TCP slow-start / scheduling spikes

Validated behaviour

  • Even at 900 Mbps offered, UE throughput capped at ~100 Mbps UL & DL
  • TCP adapts and stabilises at the cap
  • UDP excess dropped (DL) / buffered-dropped (UL)
  • No oversubscription; 1 Gbps backhaul protected; repeatable results

Related hardware gotcha: when I throttled the backhaul to 100 Mbps, the femto wouldn't come up while its port was set to speed/duplex = auto. Manually forcing 100 Mbps / full duplex at the femto brought it up, after which voice/data ran fine (≈80 Mbps UL / 92 Mbps DL on the 100 Mbps link).

Case study — multi-UE fairness & UDP throughput

Multi-UE throughput >500 Mbps — only one user served

Pushing >500 Mbps total across two UEs simultaneously, only the first UE received throughput; the second got none. Root cause was the femto's user-plane fast-path — Vector Packet Processing (VPP) + hardware accelerator offload — not handling the load correctly. Once VPP/HW-accelerator handling was fixed, both UEs were served fairly under the same offered load.

UDP capacity & the 3 Gbps overshoot

Three UEs pushed 1 Gbps each (3 Gbps total) against a 1 Gbps femto cap — the third UE got nothing. This was a test-method issue, not a femto defect: the total offered load exceeded the femto's 1 Gbps capacity. Re-running with equal UDP shares summing to under 1 Gbps gave fair distribution across all three UEs.

VPP & hardware accelerator — what these are

Vector Packet Processing (VPP) is a user-plane data-plane technique that processes vectors of packets (rather than one at a time) through a graph of nodes, drastically reducing per-packet overhead and improving throughput. Combined with hardware accelerators, this gives modern small cells / UPFs the multi-Gbps user-plane performance they need. When VPP or the offload path mis-handles a flow, the symptom is exactly what we saw: high offered load but poor or asymmetric throughput across users, while CPU appears under-utilized. Fixing the fast-path restores fair, line-rate behaviour.

Case study — low UL throughput because the UE was holding back UL power

Symptom
On a 5G SA Femto load test we saw UL throughput well below expectation at cell-mid and cell-edge, while DL throughput was healthy on the same UE. The radio environment looked fine in the femto’s point of view, the scheduler was granting the UE PRBs, but the UE just wasn’t pushing enough bits per slot.
Investigation
UL throughput was limited because the UE was transmitting at a lower power than its available capability, likely due to a UE-side power-saving policy. This resulted in low UL SINR at the gNB, forcing the scheduler to use a lower MCS and reducing throughput. TPC (Transmit Power Control) commands were being sent correctly (the network was telling the UE how much power to use), BSR (Buffer Status Reports) were being sent correctly (the UE was telling the network it had data to send), and PRB (Physical Resource Block) allocation was healthy (the UE was getting enough radio resources). The UE was simply choosing to transmit quietly.
Fix
Adjusted the UL open-loop power-control parameters on the femto side — the cell-configured target power the UE uses to compute its UL transmit power. NR uplink open-loop power control is governed by: 
PUE = P0 + α · PL + Δ
where:
  • P0 (p0-NominalWithGrant) — cell-configured baseline uplink power target. This is the knob that was raised.
  • α (alpha) — path-loss compensation factor (0…1) that decides how much of the measured path-loss the UE should compensate for.
  • PL — measured path loss between the UE and the cell.
  • Δ — additional adjustments (closed-loop TPC accumulator, MCS offsets, etc.), all bounded by the UE’s PMAX.
With P0 raised on the femto, the UE’s open-loop calculation came out higher, the UE transmitted at a higher power, UL SINR improved at the gNB, the scheduler granted a higher MCS, and UL throughput recovered.
Concept & takeaway
5G NR UL power = open-loop target (p0, alpha, path-loss) + closed-loop TPC accumulator + UE’s own implementation behaviour, bounded by PMAX. If the UE is choosing to transmit below the headroom, the network-side fix is to lift the open-loop target so the UE’s calculation comes out higher — not to send more TPC commands. Takeaway: when UL throughput is low but DL is healthy and the UE has signal, look at UE UL transmit power before you blame scheduling.

Congestion-control & long-duration validation

  • Modern congestion features I validated:
    • RPS (Resource Priority Scheduling) — confirmed slice users on the priority slices were getting roughly the configured multiplier of the normal-UE throughput (e.g. 2× or 5× the per-UE share depending on the slice spec).
    • L4S (Low-Latency, Low-Loss, Scalable throughput) — under congestion, normal UEs showed high latency and visible packet drops, while L4S UEs showed low latency, low jitter and no drops — i.e. consistent end-to-end behaviour for latency-sensitive flows.
    • ECN (Explicit Congestion Notification) — critical enabler for L4S. ECN bits in the IP header signal congestion before packets are dropped. Codepoints 01 / 10 mark packets as ECN-capable; codepoint 11 marks "congestion experienced". The femto reads and forwards these markings so the application above the stack can react (rate-shape down) instead of waiting for a packet loss — which is what lets L4S deliver low loss + low latency at the same time.
    • AQM (Active Queue Management) — needed so L4S and normal traffic sit in separate queues; for L4S-style voice and other latency-sensitive flows the femto’s AQM discards packets older than the per-flow delay budget, because for those services a late packet is worse than a dropped one.
    • RP (Resource Partitioning) — caps the maximum percentage of the cell’s RBs that a given slice is allowed to use, so one slice can’t starve the others under load.
    • Minimum bit-rate per flow — verified under congestion conditions.
  • Message segmentation under load: confirmed large messages are segmented into protocol-compliant chunks without loss or corruption.
  • Soak/stability: 8-hour VoNR calls, 24-hour data, and combined data + ViNR + VoNR — each across cell-centre, cell-mid and cell-edge.
  • Throughput matrix: TCP/UDP/HTTP UL & DL at cell-centre/mid/edge using iPerf, pingtools, and Ookla.
  • VoNR setup-time: measured across 10 calls per location (centre/mid/edge) by timing SIP INVITE → 180 Ringing.

Load & stress femto KPI analysis — what I read from the report

For every load/stress run on the femto I review the full KPI report rather than a single number. The five things I always correlate against the offered load profile:

  • Accessibility: RRC setup success ratio, UE-context setup success ratio, QoS-flow / PDU-session establishment success ratio — do new UEs still get on as load grows?
  • Retainability / mobility: RRC re-establishment ratio, drop rate, mobility (Xn/NG) HO success — do existing UEs stay up under stress?
  • Resource utilization: CPU utilization, PRB utilization (DL/UL), scheduler queue / backlog — where the cell starts saturating.
  • Core / context counters: NG context count, UE context count — growth pattern should track offered UE load.
  • Throughput & traffic: aggregate DL/UL throughput and per-UE distribution; PDCP/RLC/MAC volume; correlated against backhaul cap.

Why this matters: a campaign can hit its peak throughput target and still fail the run — e.g. on rising RRC re-establishments, on PRB saturation that starves new attaches, or on a CPU climb that ends in a reboot during soak. Reading all five together against the offered load is what distinguishes “it ran fast” from “it’s deployable”.

Field notes & common pitfalls

  • The tool defines the number. iPerf (controlled) vs Ookla (bursty/peak) give different "throughput" — pick the one matching the question.
  • Force port speed/duplex when throttling. Auto-negotiation can stop a femto coming up on a capped link.
  • Watch the aggregate cap. Per-UE targets that sum past the cell/backhaul limit starve the last UE — by design.
  • Stability ≠ throughput. A run can hit target rates yet fail on CPU/PRB or a crash — always read the KPI report, not just the speed number.
