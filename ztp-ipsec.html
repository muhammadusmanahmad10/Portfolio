Zero-Touch Provisioning & IPsec

A 5G SA femto is plug-and-play: plug it into any internet connection and it provisions itself — builds secure tunnels, pulls its configuration, validates its physical location, and comes on air with no manual setup. That is Zero-Touch Provisioning (ZTP), and it rides on IPsec tunnels to the operator. I validate the whole bring-up, the geo-security that locks it to an approved location, and the certificate lifecycle.

ZTP bring-up sequence

On power-up, the femto enrolls for an operator certificate via CMP / CMPv2 (per TS 33.310) and establishes a temporary IPsec tunnel to the Security Gateway. Through this tunnel, it receives its RF and IP configuration (PCI, TAC, NR-ARFCN, power, neighbours, NMS / 5GC / SeGW details) and validates its GPS location against the operator-authorised location. If the location check passes (≤20 m), the femto creates two permanent IPsec tunnels — one to the NMS for OAM and one to the 5GC — and removes the temporary tunnel. The NMS then provisions BMC / LMF / GMLC mappings, after which the femto activates its cells using the provisioned RF, IP, and neighbour configuration. Validation confirms correct NMS provisioning and successful cell activation.

Geo-security — the 20 m location lock

Because the femto is portable, it must not work outside its authorised spot. I validated that the femto checks its position and stays in service only within a 20 m radius — move it beyond 20 m and it is automatically blocked for security. Certificate lifecycle

IPsec to the operator is certificate-authenticated, so I validate the device initial certificate enrollment — the femto obtaining its operator-issued identity certificate on first bring-up. A broken enrollment means no tunnel and therefore no service, so this is a core part of turn-up validation.

Common IPsec / transport faults I worked through

Traffic-selector mismatch (one neighbour unreachable over 5GC tunnel)

The problem was limited to reachability of one neighbour site over the 5GC IPsec tunnel. The neighbour’s IP belonged to the 42.0.0.0/8 subnet, while the femto’s 5GC traffic selector was configured for 10.0.0.0/8. Since the destination IP did not match the configured traffic selector, the traffic was not forwarded into the IPsec tunnel and was silently dropped. Updating the traffic selector to include the neighbour’s subnet restored connectivity.

Case study — UDP checksum altered in transit (NAT-T behaviour)

Quick context — what NAT, NAT-T and ESP are:

  • NAT (Network Address Translation) — a device in the middle (router / firewall) that rewrites the IP addresses (and sometimes ports) of packets as they cross it — e.g. translating a private 10.x IP into a public one. Any time a NAT sits between two peers, the IP/port the receiver sees is not what the sender sent.
    Why this translation is needed: public IPv4 addresses are limited and expensive, so devices on home / enterprise / mobile networks are given private IPs (RFC 1918: 10.x.x.x, 172.16–31.x, 192.168.x) that are not routable on the public Internet. NAT lets many such private devices share one public IP by translating their addresses on the way out and tracking the mapping for return traffic — saving address space and, as a side benefit, hiding the internal network from the outside.
  • ESP (Encapsulating Security Payload) — the IPsec packet format that actually carries the encrypted and integrity-protected payload. ESP runs over its own IP protocol number (50), not over TCP or UDP — which is exactly the problem when there’s a NAT in the path, because most NATs only know how to translate TCP / UDP.
  • NAT-T (NAT Traversal, RFC 3947 / 3948) — the fix: when the two IPsec peers detect a NAT between them, they wrap each ESP packet inside a UDP packet on port 4500, so the NAT sees a normal UDP flow and can translate it. The ESP is exactly the same inside; it’s just inside a UDP envelope.
  • IKE proposals — the security parameters the two peers exchange during IKE negotiation that decide how the tunnel will be protected. A proposal lists the candidate encryption algorithm (e.g. AES-CBC-256, AES-GCM-256), integrity algorithm (e.g. SHA-256, SHA-384), Diffie-Hellman group (e.g. group 14 / 19 / 20) and authentication method (certificate, PSK).
Symptom
At one site the femto’s IPsec association to the SeGW would not establish even though the IKE proposals matched, IP was reachable, and the same femto build worked at other locations.
Investigation
Packet capture at both ends showed IPsec was running in NAT-T mode (UDP 4500) because of a NAT in the transport path. At the SeGW egress the UDP-encap ESP packets carried UDP checksum = 0 (allowed by RFC 3948 because ESP already covers integrity). At the femto ingress the same packets arrived with a non-zero UDP checksum — the in-path NAT had recomputed it after rewriting the source IP. The femto’s IPsec stack was strict, only accepted UDP checksum = 0 for UDP-encap ESP, and silently dropped the packets — so no IKE error appeared in the logs.
Root cause
A NAT device between SeGW and femto was recomputing the UDP checksum to non-zero (standard NAT behaviour after a source-IP rewrite), even though the SeGW was correctly sending checksum = 0. The femto’s strict UDP-checksum check then rejected every packet before the ESP layer ever saw it.
Fix & takeaway
Patched the femto IPsec stack to accept UDP-encap ESP regardless of the UDP checksum value, aligning it with RFC 3948 receiver guidance. Tunnel came up on the next attempt with no change at the SeGW or NAT. Concept: for NAT-T, the UDP checksum is a transport annotation, not a security check — ESP carries the integrity. A NAT in the path will usually recompute it to non-zero, so a receiver that insists on zero will silently break IPsec under any NAT — with no IKE error to point at.

Case study — OAM tunnel hijacked by the core tunnel’s traffic selector

Symptom
At one location the femto came up cleanly — OAM tunnel established first, then the core (5GC) tunnel came up — and both tunnels worked. The problem appeared later: any time the OAM tunnel went down (e.g. brief drop, expiry of its lifetime, IKE rekey window), it could not re-establish as long as the core tunnel was still up. The femto would lose NMS connectivity until the core tunnel was also bounced.
Investigation
The femto maintains separate IPsec tunnels for OAM and 5GC, each with its own traffic selector. The issue occurred because the 5GC tunnel was configured with an overly broad traffic selector (10.0.0.0/8), which included the private DNS server used to resolve the OAM SeGW FQDN. Once the 5GC tunnel came up, DNS queries were incorrectly routed through the 5GC tunnel instead of the OAM tunnel, causing FQDN resolution to fail and preventing OAM tunnel re-establishment. Narrowing the 5GC traffic selector restored correct DNS routing and OAM connectivity.
Root cause
A traffic-selector overlap between two SAs on the same femto: the core tunnel’s broad 10.0.0.0/8 swallowed the IP of the OAM-side DNS server. This is a classic "too-broad selector" problem — the SA chosen for a packet is whichever selector matches first, regardless of which tunnel it logically belongs to.
Fix & takeaway
Tightened the core tunnel’s traffic selector to exclude the OAM DNS subnet (or moved the DNS server outside 10.0.0.0/8), so DNS queries for the OAM SeGW were no longer captured by the core SA. The OAM tunnel could then re-resolve and re-establish cleanly even while the core tunnel stayed up. Concept: when a femto has more than one IPsec SA, the traffic selectors must be disjoint. The broadest selector wins by SAD (Security Association Database) lookup order — an aggressive /8 on one tunnel will silently steal traffic that belonged to another, and the symptom often only shows up on the second tunnel’s rekey / re-establishment, not at initial bring-up.

Case study — DPD timeout because liveness packets weren’t high-priority enough

Quick context — IKE SA vs Child SA: an IKE SA is the control-plane security association used for authentication, key exchange, and management of the IPsec connection. A Child SA is the data-plane security association that carries encrypted traffic using ESP. The IKE SA negotiates and manages one or more Child SAs, while the Child SAs are responsible for protecting the actual user, OAM, or core-network traffic.

Symptom
Under sustained high load / congestion the IPsec tunnel went down on a DPD timeout. Once the femto tore the SA down on its side, the AMF was no longer reachable and the cells went down; the femto then re-initiated IKE_SA_INIT, rebuilt the tunnel, and service came back — only to repeat the pattern the next time the cell was driven into congestion.
How DPD actually behaves
DPD (Dead Peer Detection) is an IKE liveness mechanism that uses empty INFORMATIONAL exchanges over the existing IKE SA. The femto periodically sends a liveness probe to the SeGW and expects a response within a configured retry window. If no response is received after the retry limit, the femto declares the peer unreachable and tears down the IKE SA and associated Child SAs.
Investigation
Looked at the IP header of the femto’s DPD packets in Wireshark — specifically the DSCP / DiffServ Code Point field (shown as IP → Differentiated Services Field). The DPD probes were leaving the femto with a lower priority class than network-control traffic (best-effort rather than CS6). Under congestion the transport queue dropped these DPD probes first, so the SeGW never saw them — and no INFORMATIONAL response was ever generated. From the femto’s point of view this looked like the SeGW had gone silent, so it concluded the peer was dead and tore down its SA. The SeGW was actually healthy and the IPsec data path would have stayed up if the probes had survived the queue.
Root cause
A DSCP marking gap on the femto’s outgoing IKE / DPD traffic: liveness probes were not classified as network-control priority, so they had the same drop probability as user traffic during congestion. Wireshark’s "Differentiated Services Field" inside the IPv4 header is the field that shows the DSCP value of those packets.
Fix & takeaway
Tagged the femto’s IKE / DPD outgoing traffic with the appropriate high-priority DSCP class (typically CS6 / network-control) and ensured the transport path honoured that marking. DPD probes survived the congestion window, the femto kept seeing INFORMATIONAL responses in time, no false “peer dead” was declared, and the cells stopped flapping during load tests. Concept: IPsec liveness is fragile under congestion when its packets compete with user data for queue space — if the probes get dropped, the side running the DPD timer reaches its retry limit and brings its own SA down, even though the peer was never actually dead. The right DSCP class for keepalives / DPD is network-control, not best-effort.

Why the plug-and-play model matters (deployment context)

This internet-delivered femto is ideal where Wi-Fi exists but VoWiFi isn't viable — e.g. enterprises where guests can't access corporate Wi-Fi, employees prefer not to join it, or the company applies DSCP marking that permits browsing but not VoWiFi. The femto delivers licensed 5G SA voice/data without depending on the enterprise Wi-Fi policy. Understanding this context is what lets me prioritise the test cases that actually matter for the deployment.

PTP / GPS sync validation — methods and handover

Timing on a 5G SA femto can come from GPS at the site or PTP over the IP transport. I exercised both as primary and as fallback, on the two physical setups the lab supports:

  • Method 1 — PTP from a Nokia IXR-e2 router (boundary clock / grandmaster). The IXR-e2 acted as the PTP source on the transport leg into the femto; verified PTP sync state, recovered phase/frequency at the femto and full femto-RAN functionality (attach, mobility, voice, data) with the IXR-e2 as the clock source.
  • Method 2 — PTP from a dedicated PTP switch with an attached grandmaster. Cabling: PoE in, Internet uplink, and the femto’s timing/data cable all terminated on the same PTP-capable switch. Same end-to-end RAN functionality validated with the switch + GM as the PTP source.
  • GPS as clock source. Re-ran the same femto-RAN functional pass with GPS at the site as the primary clock instead of PTP, to confirm parity of behaviour across the two clock sources.
  • Site-to-site handover. Validated the femto’s timing handover between two adjacent sites in the lab. (a) PTP → PTP: one site configured with PTP as its clock source and the other site also configured with PTP; the UE was handed over between the two sites and timing on the target side stayed locked through the handover. (b) PTP → GPS: one site configured with PTP and the other site configured with GPS as the clock source; ran the same site-to-site handover and verified the UE moved cleanly from the PTP-timed cell to the GPS-timed cell with no service drop.

Why this matters: 5G NR TDD needs cell-to-cell phase alignment well under ±1.5 µs, so the timing path is part of the femto’s service definition. Exercising PTP from two different physical paths (router-as-source vs PTP-switch-with-GM) plus GPS, and proving clean site-to-site handover for PTP → PTP and PTP → GPS, is what closes the timing test plan before lab exit.

IPsec / IKE building blocks I validate

ElementWhat it is & what I check
IKE SA (IKESA)The control-plane Security Association negotiated by IKE_SA_INIT — it carries the keying material, DH group, encryption / integrity algorithms and a SPI pair (SPI = Security Parameter Index, a unique identifier the peers exchange — one per direction — so each side knows which SA to apply to incoming packets; the IKE SPI is 8 bytes in IKEv2, the ESP/Child-SA SPI is 4 bytes) used to protect all subsequent IKE messages. I check that IKE_SA_INIT completes, the negotiated proposals match the SeGW, and that rekey fires before the lifetime expires without dropping the IKE channel.
IKE_AUTHThe second exchange that authenticates the peers and creates the first Child SA. I verify the femto presents the correct device certificate, and that the SeGW chain is validated against the trusted CA.
Child SA (IPsec SA)The actual data-plane SA created under the IKE SA. A Child SA negotiation produces two unidirectional SAs (one for each direction), each with its own SPI, keys and selectors. I verify that the right Child SA covers temp / NMS / 5GC traffic as designed and that rekey (time- or byte-based) works without packet loss.
Traffic Selectors (TSi / TSr)The address ranges that say what traffic the Child SA protects. I match the femto's local selector to the peer subnets — the 10.0.0.0 vs 42.0.0.0 case was exactly a TS mismatch.
Encryption / Integrity proposale.g. AES-GCM-256 / AES-CBC-256 + SHA-256 / SHA-384. I verify proposal alignment between peers — one mismatched transform in the proposal kills IKE_SA_INIT.
DH group / PFSDH group used for IKE SA and for Child SA rekey (PFS = Perfect Forward Secrecy — an IPsec feature that uses a fresh Diffie-Hellman exchange during Child SA creation or rekeying to generate new encryption keys independently of previous keys, so that if one session key is compromised, past and future sessions remain secure). I confirm the group is enabled on both sides (e.g. group 14 / 19 / 20) and that PFS-on-rekey behaves.
DPD (Dead Peer Detection)Liveness probes over the IKE SA. I tune interval / retries and reproduce the load-induced DPD timeout case so the SA tears down cleanly and rebuilds rather than going zombie.
NAT-T (RFC 3947 / 3948)UDP-encapsulated ESP on port 4500 when a NAT is in the path. I confirm NAT-D payload detection, the keepalive cadence, and that the femto behind enterprise NAT still rekeys cleanly.
Device certificate & CA chainOperator-issued cert (CMPv2). I check enrollment on first bring-up, the chain to the operator Root CA, and that revocation (CRL) is honoured.

Field notes & common pitfalls

  • Location gate is a hard dependency. Permanent tunnels (and service) only come up after the ≤20 m check passes.
  • Factory reset ≠ reboot. Only factory reset clears config and re-triggers ZTP — important when reproducing field issues.
  • Certificates are silent killers. Enrollment failures look like generic "no service"; check the tunnel and cert state first.
  • Traffic selectors must match end-to-end — a pingable path can still drop the tunnel's protected traffic.
