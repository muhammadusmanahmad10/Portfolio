Zero-Touch Provisioning & IPsec

A 5G SA femto is plug-and-play: plug it into any internet connection and it provisions itself — builds secure tunnels, pulls its configuration, validates its physical location, and comes on air with no manual setup. That is Zero-Touch Provisioning (ZTP), and it rides on IPsec tunnels to the operator. I validate the whole bring-up, the geo-security that locks it to an approved location, and the certificate lifecycle.

ZTP bring-up sequence

On power-up the femto first performs device certificate enrollment with the operator’s Registration Authority over CMP / CMPv2 (per TS 33.310) to obtain its operator-issued certificate. Using that operator cert, the femto then opens one temporary IPsec tunnel to the security gateway. Through the temp tunnel it reaches the operator’s provisioning platform group — the central system that holds the authorised location for this cell and pushes back the cell’s RF data (PCI, TAC, NR-ARFCN, power, neighbour relations) and IP data (NMS IPs, 5GC IPs, SeGW endpoints / FQDN). The femto then reports its actual GPS location and that’s compared against the authorised location in the operator platform — if it’s outside the ≤20 m window the femto is blocked at this gate. Once location passes, the femto builds its two permanent tunnels — one to the NMS (for ongoing OAM) and one to the 5G Core — both authenticated with the enrolled operator cert; the temporary tunnel is then torn down as it’s no longer needed. Over the NMS permanent tunnel, the NMS then provisions the femto’s BMC / LMF / GMLC bindings (broadcast / positioning / gateway-mobile-location mapping) — the operator-provisioning platform group has no role at this stage; BMC/LMF/GMLC delivery is the NMS’s job. The femto then activates the cells with the provisioned RF + IP configuration and neighbour relations; I verify the NMS correctly delivers the spectrum / BMC / LMF / GMLC pieces and that the femto comes up as expected.

Geo-security — the 20 m location lock

Because the femto is portable, it must not work outside its authorised spot. I validated that the femto checks its position and stays in service only within a 20 m radius — move it beyond 20 m and it is automatically blocked for security. Certificate lifecycle

IPsec to the operator is certificate-authenticated, so I validate the device initial certificate enrollment — the femto obtaining its operator-issued identity certificate on first bring-up. A broken enrollment means no tunnel and therefore no service, so this is a core part of turn-up validation.

Common IPsec / transport faults I worked through

Traffic-selector mismatch (one neighbour unreachable over 5GC tunnel)

This didn’t block bring-up — the femto came up cleanly. The symptom was that one specific neighbour site was unreachable over the 5G Core IPsec tunnel: that neighbour’s IP was in 42.0.0.0/8, but the femto’s 5GC traffic selector was configured as 10.0.0.0/8. Because the neighbour’s destination didn’t match the SA’s selector, the femto never sent that traffic into the tunnel — so packets to that one neighbour were silently dropped while everything else worked fine. Aligning the traffic selector to cover the neighbour’s subnet restored reachability.

DPD timeout under load

Under high load the IPsec tunnel went down on a DPD timeout. DPD (Dead Peer Detection) runs on a timer — the femto periodically sends a small IKE liveness probe to the SeGW and expects a response back within a configured retry window. Root cause was a DSCP marking gap: the DPD probes were leaving the femto with a lower priority class than network-control traffic, so under congestion the transport queue dropped them first. With no response coming back in time, the femto itself declared the SeGW unreachable and tore the IKE / Child SAs down from its side — even though the SeGW was healthy and the IPsec data path would otherwise have been fine. With the tunnel gone, the femto cells went out of service; the femto then re-initiated IKE_SA_INIT, rebuilt the tunnel, and service came back. Fixed by tagging the IKE / DPD traffic with the correct high-priority DSCP (network-control class). Detailed write-up in the case section below.

Case study — UDP checksum altered in transit (NAT-T behaviour)

Quick context — what NAT, NAT-T and ESP are:

  • NAT (Network Address Translation) — a device in the middle (router / firewall) that rewrites the IP addresses (and sometimes ports) of packets as they cross it — e.g. translating a private 10.x IP into a public one. Any time a NAT sits between two peers, the IP/port the receiver sees is not what the sender sent.
    Why this translation is needed: public IPv4 addresses are limited and expensive, so devices on home / enterprise / mobile networks are given private IPs (RFC 1918: 10.x.x.x, 172.16–31.x, 192.168.x) that are not routable on the public Internet. NAT lets many such private devices share one public IP by translating their addresses on the way out and tracking the mapping for return traffic — saving address space and, as a side benefit, hiding the internal network from the outside.
  • ESP (Encapsulating Security Payload) — the IPsec packet format that actually carries the encrypted and integrity-protected payload. ESP runs over its own IP protocol number (50), not over TCP or UDP — which is exactly the problem when there’s a NAT in the path, because most NATs only know how to translate TCP / UDP.
  • NAT-T (NAT Traversal, RFC 3947 / 3948) — the fix: when the two IPsec peers detect a NAT between them, they wrap each ESP packet inside a UDP packet on port 4500, so the NAT sees a normal UDP flow and can translate it. The ESP is exactly the same inside; it’s just inside a UDP envelope.
  • IKE proposals — the security parameters the two peers exchange during IKE negotiation that decide how the tunnel will be protected. A proposal lists the candidate encryption algorithm (e.g. AES-CBC-256, AES-GCM-256), integrity algorithm (e.g. SHA-256, SHA-384), Diffie-Hellman group (e.g. group 14 / 19 / 20) and authentication method (certificate, PSK).
Symptom
At one site the femto’s IPsec association to the SeGW would not establish even though the IKE proposals matched, IP was reachable, and the same femto build worked in other locations.
Investigation
While capturing traffic between the femto and the SeGW, I found that IPsec was using NAT-T (UDP port 4500) because a NAT device was in the path. According to the NAT-T standard, UDP-encapsulated ESP packets should have a UDP checksum of 0. However, in the packet capture, the UDP checksum was non-zero. The receiving side treated these packets as invalid and dropped them because they did not match the expected NAT-T packet format. As a result, the IPsec tunnel could not be established properly. Specifically, the SeGW was sending packets to the femto, and the femto was discarding them because the UDP checksum field wasn’t the expected zero.
Why the UDP checksum is recommended to be zero (and why non-zero is also normal)
For UDP-encapsulated ESP, RFC 3948 recommends the sender set the UDP checksum field to 0x0000. The reason is that ESP already provides its own integrity check over the inner payload — computing a second UDP checksum on top is redundant, and it would also be wrong after any NAT, because NAT changes the addresses / ports that the UDP checksum is computed over. So “UDP checksum = 0” doesn’t mean “no integrity”; it means “don’t use this field, trust ESP’s own integrity instead.”
But a non-zero UDP checksum is also perfectly normal: although RFC 3948 allows UDP-encapsulated IPsec packets to use a UDP checksum of 0, many modern devices automatically calculate and add a normal (non-zero) UDP checksum to all UDP packets. This is standard behaviour and does not indicate a problem. The RFC also states that the receiving device should accept the packet whether the UDP checksum is zero or non-zero. Therefore, a sender using a non-zero checksum is behaving normally. If the receiver rejects those packets because the checksum is non-zero, then the issue is with the receiver being too strict — not with the sender.
Root cause
The SeGW was emitting UDP-encap ESP with a non-zero UDP checksum (normal modern UDP behaviour), but the femto’s IPsec stack was being strict and only accepting UDP-encap ESP packets where the UDP checksum was exactly 0x0000. So every packet the SeGW sent arrived at the femto with what looked to it like an “unexpected” checksum field, and the femto dropped them before the IKE / ESP layer could process anything — which is why no IKE error showed up in the logs.
Fix & takeaway
The fix was made on the femto side: its IPsec stack was updated to accept UDP-encap ESP regardless of the UDP checksum value (zero or non-zero), aligning it with the RFC 3948 receiver guidance instead of strictly enforcing checksum = 0. Once that change was in, the tunnel came up on the next attempt with the SeGW’s own packets unchanged. Concept: for NAT-T, the UDP checksum field is a transport-level annotation, not a security check — ESP carries the integrity. A non-zero checksum on UDP-encap ESP is allowed by the standard; a receiver that rejects those packets is over-strict and silently breaks IPsec without any IKE error in the logs.

Case study — OAM tunnel hijacked by the core tunnel’s traffic selector

Symptom
At one location the femto came up cleanly — OAM tunnel established first, then the core (5GC) tunnel came up — and both tunnels worked. The problem appeared later: any time the OAM tunnel went down (e.g. brief drop, expiry of its lifetime, IKE rekey window), it could not re-establish as long as the core tunnel was still up. The femto would lose NMS connectivity until the core tunnel was also bounced.
Investigation
The femto holds two permanent IPsec tunnels after bring-up: one to the NMS / OAM and one to the 5G Core. Each tunnel has its own traffic selector describing which destination prefixes belong to it. To re-establish the OAM tunnel, the femto first has to resolve the OAM-side SeGW FQDN via a private DNS server. In the offending location, that private DNS server’s IP fell inside the core tunnel’s traffic-selector prefix — the core TS had been provisioned aggressively as 10.0.0.0/8. So while only the OAM tunnel was up (initial bring-up), DNS worked fine and the OAM tunnel came up without issue. Once the core tunnel was also up, the IPsec SAD (Security Association Database) selector match on the femto picked the core tunnel’s SA for any packet destined to 10.0.0.x — including the DNS query for the OAM SeGW. The DNS query went out over the wrong SA, no response came back on the OAM path, FQDN resolution failed, and the OAM tunnel could not re-establish for as long as the core tunnel held that aggressive selector.
Root cause
A traffic-selector overlap between two SAs on the same femto: the core tunnel’s broad 10.0.0.0/8 swallowed the IP of the OAM-side DNS server. This is a classic "too-broad selector" problem — the SA chosen for a packet is whichever selector matches first, regardless of which tunnel it logically belongs to.
Fix & takeaway
Tightened the core tunnel’s traffic selector to exclude the OAM DNS subnet (or moved the DNS server outside 10.0.0.0/8), so DNS queries for the OAM SeGW were no longer captured by the core SA. The OAM tunnel could then re-resolve and re-establish cleanly even while the core tunnel stayed up. Concept: when a femto has more than one IPsec SA, the traffic selectors must be disjoint. The broadest selector wins by SAD lookup order — an aggressive /8 on one tunnel will silently steal traffic that belonged to another, and the symptom often only shows up on the second tunnel’s rekey / re-establishment, not at initial bring-up.

Case study — DPD timeout because liveness packets weren’t high-priority enough

Quick context — IKE SA vs Child SA: an IKE SA is the control-plane security association used for authentication, key exchange, and management of the IPsec connection. A Child SA is the data-plane security association that carries encrypted traffic using ESP. The IKE SA negotiates and manages one or more Child SAs, while the Child SAs are responsible for protecting the actual user, OAM, or core-network traffic.

Symptom
Under sustained high load / congestion the IPsec tunnel went down on a DPD timeout. Once the femto tore the SA down on its side, the AMF was no longer reachable and the cells went down; the femto then re-initiated IKE_SA_INIT, rebuilt the tunnel, and service came back — only to repeat the pattern the next time the cell was driven into congestion.
How DPD actually behaves
DPD (Dead Peer Detection) is a timer-based liveness check that runs over the existing IKE SA. The concept was originally specified for IKEv1 in RFC 3706; in IKEv2 (RFC 7296 §2.4) the equivalent mechanism is the empty INFORMATIONAL exchange, which is what this femto uses. Before its inactivity timer expires, the femto sends a short IKE liveness probe (an empty INFORMATIONAL request) to the SeGW and expects an INFORMATIONAL response back within a configured retry window. If the femto doesn’t see a response after the retry count, the femto itself declares the peer dead and tears the IKE SA (and its Child SAs) down from its side. Liveness is bidirectional in principle, but in this deployment the femto’s timer was tighter, so the femto was the side that gave up first.
Investigation
Looked at the IP header of the femto’s DPD packets in Wireshark — specifically the DSCP / DiffServ Code Point field (shown as IP → Differentiated Services Field). The DPD probes were leaving the femto with a lower priority class than network-control traffic (best-effort rather than CS6). Under congestion the transport queue dropped these DPD probes first, so the SeGW never saw them — and no INFORMATIONAL response was ever generated. From the femto’s point of view this looked like the SeGW had gone silent, so it concluded the peer was dead and tore down its SA. The SeGW was actually healthy and the IPsec data path would have stayed up if the probes had survived the queue.
Root cause
A DSCP marking gap on the femto’s outgoing IKE / DPD traffic: liveness probes were not classified as network-control priority, so they had the same drop probability as user traffic during congestion. Wireshark’s "Differentiated Services Field" inside the IPv4 header is the field that shows the DSCP value of those packets.
Fix & takeaway
Tagged the femto’s IKE / DPD outgoing traffic with the appropriate high-priority DSCP class (typically CS6 / network-control) and ensured the transport path honoured that marking. DPD probes survived the congestion window, the femto kept seeing INFORMATIONAL responses in time, no false “peer dead” was declared, and the cells stopped flapping during load tests. Concept: IPsec liveness is fragile under congestion when its packets compete with user data for queue space — if the probes get dropped, the side running the DPD timer reaches its retry limit and brings its own SA down, even though the peer was never actually dead. The right DSCP class for keepalives / DPD is network-control, not best-effort.

Why the plug-and-play model matters (deployment context)

This internet-delivered femto is ideal where Wi-Fi exists but VoWiFi isn't viable — e.g. enterprises where guests can't access corporate Wi-Fi, employees prefer not to join it, or the company applies DSCP marking that permits browsing but not VoWiFi. The femto delivers licensed 5G SA voice/data without depending on the enterprise Wi-Fi policy. Understanding this context is what lets me prioritise the test cases that actually matter for the deployment.

PTP / GPS sync validation — methods and handover

Timing on a 5G SA femto can come from GPS at the site or PTP over the IP transport. I exercised both as primary and as fallback, on the two physical setups the lab supports:

  • Method 1 — PTP from a Nokia IXR-e2 router (boundary clock / grandmaster). The IXR-e2 acted as the PTP source on the transport leg into the femto; verified PTP sync state, recovered phase/frequency at the femto and full femto-RAN functionality (attach, mobility, voice, data) with the IXR-e2 as the clock source.
  • Method 2 — PTP from a dedicated PTP switch with an attached grandmaster. Cabling: PoE in, Internet uplink, and the femto’s timing/data cable all terminated on the same PTP-capable switch. Same end-to-end RAN functionality validated with the switch + GM as the PTP source.
  • GPS as clock source. Re-ran the same femto-RAN functional pass with GPS at the site as the primary clock instead of PTP, to confirm parity of behaviour across the two clock sources.
  • Site-to-site handover. Validated the femto’s timing handover between two adjacent sites in the lab. (a) PTP → PTP: one site configured with PTP as its clock source and the other site also configured with PTP; the UE was handed over between the two sites and timing on the target side stayed locked through the handover. (b) PTP → GPS: one site configured with PTP and the other site configured with GPS as the clock source; ran the same site-to-site handover and verified the UE moved cleanly from the PTP-timed cell to the GPS-timed cell with no service drop. In each case the femto retained phase/frequency within the TDD budget and stayed in service through the handover.

Why this matters: 5G NR TDD needs cell-to-cell phase alignment well under ±1.5 µs, so the timing path is part of the femto’s service definition. Exercising PTP from two different physical paths (router-as-source vs PTP-switch-with-GM) plus GPS, and proving clean site-to-site handover for PTP → PTP and PTP → GPS, is what closes the timing test plan before lab exit.

I check that IKE_SA_INIT completes, the negotiated proposals match the SeGW, and that rekey fires before the lifetime expires without dropping the IKE channel.

Child SA (IPsec SA)The actual data-plane SA created under the IKE SA. A Child SA negotiation produces two unidirectional SAs (one for each direction), each with its own SPI, keys and selectors. I verify that the right Child SA covers temp / NMS / 5GC traffic as designed and that rekey (time- or byte-based) works without packet loss.
Traffic Selectors (TSi / TSr)The address ranges that say what traffic the Child SA protects. I match the femto's local selector to the peer subnets — the 10.0.0.0 vs 42.0.0.0 case was exactly a TS mismatch.

Field notes & common pitfalls

  • Location gate is a hard dependency. Permanent tunnels (and service) only come up after the ≤20 m check passes.
  • Factory reset ≠ reboot. Only factory reset clears config and re-triggers ZTP — important when reproducing field issues.
  • Certificates are silent killers. Enrollment failures look like generic "no service"; check the tunnel and cert state first.
  • Traffic selectors must match end-to-end — a pingable path can still drop the tunnel's protected traffic.
