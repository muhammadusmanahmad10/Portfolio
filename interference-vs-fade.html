Interference vs Fade Diagnosis — RSRP / RSSI / RSRQ / SINR

A dropping SINR can be any of four very different things: a coverage/fade problem, an interference problem, a receiver / noise-floor problem, or a channel-conditioning problem (correlation / rank collapse). Each demands a different fix. The way to separate them is to read RSRP, RSSI, RSRQ and SINR as a pattern, not as individual values — and to remember the identity RSRQ = N · RSRP / RSSI.

RSRP (signal) RSSI (total band) RSRQ = N·RSRP/RSSI SINR (operating) CRS-domain interference

The four definitions, in one place

  • RSRP — Reference-Signal Received Power. Per-RE power of the serving cell’s CRS, averaged over the band. Measures our signal.
  • RSSI — wideband Received Signal Strength Indicator. Total power in the measurement band including serving, neighbours and noise.
  • RSRQ — N · RSRP / RSSI where N is the number of PRBs in the measurement band. Rises when more of the band is “ours”; falls when the band is full of other stuff.
  • SINR — Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio. The quantity the eNB’s link-adaptation actually tracks and the one that sets MCS/Rank.

The four failure modes — separated by pattern

ModeRSRPRSSIRSRQSINRWhat’s happening
Coverage / fade Falls Falls (tracks RSRP) Roughly stable Roughly stable then falls at noise floor Signal and total band shrink together — path loss or body/attitude fade.
Interference-limited Stable Rises Falls (RSRP ÷ bigger RSSI) Falls Another cell is punching into the band — our signal is still there, but the band is noisier.
Receiver noise / desense Stable (equal on both branches) Equal Equal One branch deficit, ~5 dB One RX chain has elevated noise floor — RSRP matches but MIMO-rank-2 can’t hold.
Channel conditioning / correlation Stable Stable Stable Stable but rank drops Antennas de-correlate — the eigen-channel loses rank even though SINR is fine.

CRS-domain interference — the one that only RSRQ reveals

If a neighbour cell is idle (no PDSCH traffic) but still transmitting CRS, it doesn’t show up on RSSI much (no data power), but it does interfere on CRS REs — and CRS is what RSRP and channel estimation ride on. The signature:

  • RSRP stable, RSSI stable, RSRQ depressed, SINR depressed on PDSCH — especially when the neighbour has the same PCI mod-3 (see PCI Planning).
  • Terrestrial drive-test tools often miss this because they log RSSI in a high-traffic hour and the band looks busy for many reasons. On ATG’s SFN, mod-3 PCI conflicts are the typical cause.

Internal vs external interference

  • Internal — another cell on the same operator, same carrier. Shows up on one or two specific geographic zones that correspond to pairs of cells. Fixable by PCI re-plan, tilt, power or frame offset.
  • External — an emitter from outside the network (another service, PIM, repeater). Shows up broadly or on specific sub-bands. Online vs offline FFT scan is the standard separator: see RF Optimization & KPIs.
  • Receiver-side — symmetric-RSRP but asymmetric-SINR across branches. Points to the radio chain (LNA / filter / cable / connector) on one branch. Bench NF/sensitivity test.

Diagnostic reading order I always use

  1. Look at RSRP first — is it stable or falling?
  2. Look at RSSI next — does it track RSRP, rise against it, or sit flat?
  3. Compute RSRQ from the identity and sanity-check the measurement.
  4. Compare SINR per branch (V and H) — symmetric or deficit?
  5. Check rank/PMI to separate channel-conditioning from everything else.
  6. Only then open the scheduler / OLLA / DCI traces. Most SINR questions are answered before step 6.

Pitfalls I stay aware of

  • Treating RSRP drop as the explanation — it may be coincidence; check RSSI/RSRQ.
  • Missing CRS-domain interference because RSSI looks fine during a quiet-hour measurement.
  • Blaming the far cell for a mod-3 conflict on the near neighbour.
  • Chasing noise-floor asymmetry on the network side when the deficit is actually a bench-level receiver-chain issue.
