Lab Interference Testing (Spirent / Vertex) & RoT

Lab validation of ATG loads has to reproduce the one condition that flight test exposes most: elevated interference. I use Spirent / Vertex channel emulators to inject UL / DL interference at calibrated levels — defined via noise-floor math and the Rise-over-Thermal (RoT) convention — then verify the injection landed by reading eNB UL RSSI. Getting the math right is what separates a meaningful lab result from a decorative one.

Noise-floor math Bandwidth correction C/N convention RoT (normal/moderate/heavy) UL RSSI verification

Noise-floor math — the starting line

  • Thermal noise floor: −174 dBm/Hz at 290 K.
  • Bandwidth correction: + 10 · log10(BW_Hz). For 1 Hz → 0 dB, for 180 kHz (one RB) → +52.6 dB, for 10 MHz → +70 dB.
  • Receiver noise figure: + NF. ATG eNB NF is typically a few dB.
  • Total noise floor = −174 + 10·log10(BW) + NF.
  • Example: ATG UL 6 RB (1.08 MHz) with NF 4 dB → floor ≈ −174 + 60.3 + 4 = −109.7 dBm.

Bandwidth correction — 6-RB vs 10 MHz

A classic mistake is to quote noise power at 10 MHz when the ATG UL is only 6 RB. The correction:

  • 10 MHz floor — use for comparability with terrestrial spec sheets.
  • 6-RB floor — use for ATG-specific budget calculations. 10·log10(10 MHz / 1.08 MHz) ≈ 9.7 dB lower floor than the 10 MHz figure.
  • Always state the bandwidth you’re computing noise over — a dB figure without a BW is undefined.

The C/N convention — which denominator?

  • C/N — Carrier-to-Noise. Numerator = signal power as delivered, denominator = thermal noise floor (optionally with NF).
  • C/(N+I) — add interference to the denominator. This is what the eNB actually experiences in the lab and in the air.
  • SINR as reported by the eNB uses C/(N+I) and is what link adaptation tracks.
  • When writing lab test cases, be explicit: “C/N at −10 dB” means C at noise-floor minus 10 dB, which is a different setup than “C/(N+I) at −10 dB”.

RoT — Rise over Thermal

RoT measures how far the total UL received power on the eNB has risen above the thermal noise floor — i.e. how “hot” the UL band is from UE transmissions, interference and noise.

RoT bandTypical dBOperating meaning
Normal0–3 dBLightly loaded; most UL budget spent on noise.
Moderate3–6 dBLoaded cell; scheduler closer to its limits; UL throughput sensitive to additional interference.
Heavy> 6 dBUL near the stability edge; additional interference causes rapid degradation. Terrestrial networks typically cap here.

Injecting interference — UL and DL

  • DL interference — Spirent / Vertex injects into the UE’s RX path, simulating a neighbour cell’s CRS/PDSCH leaking into the serving cell. Validates how the UE reports CQI and how the eNB reacts via OLLA.
  • UL interference — inject into the eNB’s RX path, simulating other UEs or external emitters in the UL band. Validates RoT behaviour, scheduler fairness and UL BLER response.
  • Calibrated levels — set injection relative to the eNB’s thermal floor, not an absolute dBm value. Absolute-dBm setups drift as the lab chain changes.
  • Verification — read the eNB’s UL RSSI counter before and after injection. Delta should match the intended RoT lift. If it doesn’t, the injection didn’t land where expected — cables, attenuators or combiners.

A sane lab protocol

  1. Measure baseline UL RSSI with no injection — this is your empirical thermal floor + residual lab noise.
  2. Compute the required injected-power level for the target RoT (e.g. +3 dB or +6 dB over baseline).
  3. Inject, re-read UL RSSI, confirm the delta.
  4. Only then run the DUT test case — the test result is only meaningful if the interference actually landed.
  5. Document injected level, bandwidth, pattern (CW, AWGN, modulated) and verification RSSI.

Pitfalls I stay aware of

  • Quoting noise power without a bandwidth. Undefined.
  • Setting injection in absolute dBm. Hidden lab-chain changes drift the intended C/N without warning.
  • Mixing C/N with C/(N+I) in the same test plan.
  • Skipping the UL RSSI verification step. The injection may be entirely absent.
  • Pushing RoT into Heavy and reporting results without noting that terrestrial networks wouldn’t operate there — the data is useful but needs context.
