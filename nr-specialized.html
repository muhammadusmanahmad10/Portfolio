5G NR Specialized Features

ℹ️Knowledge page. Specialized NR features standardised in Rel-16/17/18 — included for completeness alongside the hands-on deployment work covered elsewhere in the portfolio.

Most of 5G is about everyday phone traffic — voice, video, browsing — called eMBB. On top of that, 3GPP adds a handful of specialized features for use-cases where eMBB isn’t the right fit: things that need near-zero latency, sharing spectrum with LTE, gNBs that backhaul over the air, one-to-many broadcasts, direct phone-to-phone radio, or huge fleets of low-power sensors. This page explains each one in plain language.

URLLCDSS IABNR MBS mMTC concepts

URLLC — Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communications

In one line: the “don’t-drop-a-single-packet, deliver-it-now” mode of 5G — used where a late or lost packet has real-world consequences (factory robot, surgical tool, smart-grid breaker). Target: about 1 ms latency and one error per 100,000 packets.

How NR pulls it off — in plain words:

  • Mini-slot scheduling — instead of waiting for a full slot, the scheduler can send a short 2/4/7-symbol mini-slot so a packet starts and finishes much faster.
  • PDCP duplication — send the same packet twice over different paths (different cells or carriers); the receiver keeps the first copy that arrives correctly. Belt-and-braces reliability.
  • Configured Grant / SPS (Semi-Persistent Scheduling) — pre-book uplink resources for the UE so it can transmit immediately, without first asking and waiting.
  • Aggressive HARQ target — aim for almost-zero errors on the first transmission, instead of the usual ~10% with retransmissions.
  • Pre-emption — if URLLC traffic shows up mid-slot, it can steal resources from regular eMBB traffic.
  • Compact DCI (Downlink Control Information) — smaller / faster scheduling messages so control decoding doesn’t add latency.

3GPP context: introduced in Rel-15, enhanced in Rel-16 (Industrial IoT) and Rel-17.

DSS — Dynamic Spectrum Sharing

In one line: let LTE and 5G NR ride the same MHz of spectrum, alternating slot-by-slot, instead of forcing the operator to clear a band before launching 5G. The scheduler decides on the fly: this millisecond is LTE, the next is NR. NR rate-matches its data around LTE’s cell-specific reference signals (CRS), and LTE marks some sub-frames as MBSFN-like to free symbols for NR. Why it matters: an operator can light up 5G on a low / mid band while LTE still carries the bulk of the traffic — no painful refarming and no users left behind. 3GPP Rel-15+.

IAB — Integrated Access & Backhaul

In one line: a gNB that uses the NR air interface itself as its backhaul — no fibre or microwave required. Especially useful at mmWave (FR2), where dropping fibre to every tiny cell on every street corner isn’t practical.

  • IAB-donor — the “anchor” gNB with real wired backhaul into the 5G core.
  • IAB-node — gNB-like node that hops back to the donor over NR (can chain multiple hops).
  • BAP (Backhaul Adaptation Protocol) + BH RLC channels route the traffic across those wireless hops.

3GPP context: Rel-16 (initial), Rel-17 (mobile IAB and enhancements).

NR MBS — Multicast / Broadcast Services

In one line: one transmission, many listeners. Instead of sending the same packet separately to a thousand UEs, the cell sends it once and they all decode it. The 5G version of LTE’s eMBMS, with two flavours: Multicast (a known group, with RRC context and feedback) and Broadcast (no UE-specific setup — anyone in the cell can pick it up). Useful for things like public-safety alerts, software updates to many devices at once, live event streaming, and V2X group safety messages. Specified in Rel-17 (TS 23.247, TS 38.300).

mMTC — massive Machine-Type Communications

In one line: the “huge fleets of tiny, cheap, battery-powered things” corner of the network — sensors, meters, trackers, wearables — that don’t need fast data but do need very low power, very low cost, and the ability to run for years on a battery. Today this is mostly served by NB-IoT and LTE-M / Cat-M on the LTE side. The NR-native equivalents are RedCap (Reduced-Capability NR, Rel-17 — for wearables and industrial sensors) and the lighter eRedCap in Rel-18. Same trade-off across the family: lower bandwidth, fewer receive antennas, longer battery life, simpler stack.

Why each matters in validation — in plain words

FeatureWhat I would actually check
URLLCIs the end-to-end latency really within the 1 ms budget? Are configured-grant resources arriving on time? Is PDCP duplication actually being deduplicated at the receiver? Is pre-emption firing when URLLC traffic shows up? Is first-transmission BLER as low as claimed?
DSSIs the NR data correctly rate-matched around the LTE CRS (so nothing collides)? Are the LTE MBSFN sub-frames actually freeing the symbols NR expects? Does LTE service stay healthy when NR traffic ramps?
IABHow much extra latency does each wireless hop add? Does BAP route traffic to the right path? Does the system fail over cleanly when a donor / node drops? Is the mmWave back-link stable enough to act as backhaul?
NR MBSCan UEs join and leave a multicast group cleanly? Does it switch correctly between multicast and broadcast? Are all UEs — including those at the cell edge — actually receiving the content?
